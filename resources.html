BJJ questions and answers

Browse by topic. Tap a question to read the answer.

Basics

What is guard in BJJ?

Guard is a bottom position where you use your legs and grips to manage distance, off-balance your partner and attack. Explore guard techniques.

What is the difference between closed guard and open guard?

In closed guard, your legs wrap around your partner with your ankles crossed. Open guard keeps the legs uncrossed and uses frames, hooks or grips to control distance. See the glossary.

What is a guard pass?

A guard pass moves around or through the bottom player’s legs to reach a controlling position such as side control. Knee cut, leg drag and toreando are examples. Browse techniques.

What is a sweep?

A sweep reverses position from guard so the bottom player comes on top. Scissor, flower and hip bump sweeps are common examples. Explore sweeps.

What is the difference between a sweep and a takedown?

A sweep starts from guard on the ground and reverses the position. A takedown brings a standing opponent to the mat. Browse the glossary.

What is the difference between gi and no-gi?

Gi training uses a jacket, pants and belt that can be gripped for control and submissions. No-gi training does not use those clothing grips. Rules can vary by event. See competition prep.

What is back control?

Back control places you behind your partner, often with hooks or a body triangle and a seatbelt grip. It creates strong opportunities for chokes. Browse techniques.

What is side control?

Side control is a top position across your partner’s torso after you pass the guard. It offers control and several routes to mount or submissions. Explore positions.

What is an armbar?

An armbar is a submission that extends the elbow joint while controlling the arm and body. Apply submissions with your training partner’s safety in mind. Explore submissions.

What is ashi garami?

Ashi garami is a family of leg entanglements used to control a leg and enter leg attacks. Specific finishes and permitted techniques depend on the ruleset. See related terms.

What should a beginner focus on first?

Start with recognizing the main positions, keeping a stable base, using frames and learning a few escapes. Ask your coach which skills to prioritize in your academy’s beginner curriculum. Browse fundamentals.

What is a frame, and how is it different from pushing?

A frame uses the alignment of your forearm, shin or another body part to maintain space. Instead of repeatedly pushing with your arms, connect your frame to hip movement so you can improve your position. Watch a frames lesson.

What does keeping your elbows close mean?

Keeping your elbows near your ribs can make it harder for a partner to isolate an arm and helps connect your arms to your defensive frames. The exact placement changes with the position; avoid treating it as a rule to stay curled up everywhere. Explore defensive positions.

What is an underhook?

An underhook places your arm underneath your partner’s arm to control the upper body. It can help you turn onto your side, come up from half guard or connect to a body lock, depending on your position. Find underhook lessons.

Positions & escapes

What is the difference between guard retention and guard recovery?

Retention keeps a passer from getting beyond your legs. Recovery puts your legs back between you and your partner after your guard has been partly or fully beaten. Both rely on managing distance and moving your hips. Explore guard lessons.

Why does my guard get passed when I reach for grips?

Reaching can leave your legs and hips unprotected. Try establishing useful leg connections and frames while you grip, and notice whether the passer is controlling your knees or turning your hips away. Study guard positions.

What should I work on when stuck under side control?

Practice protecting your arms, establishing frames and creating enough space to move your hips and recover a leg. Start with a coach-taught escape and test it in short positional rounds rather than trying to bench-press your partner. Find side-control lessons.

What are the main ideas behind escaping mount?

Two common approaches are trapping an arm and leg to bridge and roll, or using frames and hip movement to bring a knee back inside. Your partner’s balance and reactions help determine which route is available. Explore mount lessons.

What should I prioritize when someone has my back?

Protect your neck and address the choking grips before concentrating on removing hooks. Learn an escape with your coach that combines hand fighting, body positioning and turning toward a safer position. Find back-control lessons.

Why do I keep losing top position after a pass?

Getting past the legs is only part of the pass. Check whether your partner can turn toward you, insert a knee or move their hips freely. Practice settling into control before immediately reaching for a submission. Explore passing lessons.

Training & learning

How should I plan a training session?

Pick one position or skill to focus on, drill its entry and response, then try it during positional sparring. Record what worked and what needs another round of practice. Use the training guide.

What does position before submission mean?

Build enough control to keep your partner from easily escaping before committing to a finish. It is a useful learning principle: if an attack repeatedly costs you the position, work on the control and transition first. Browse positions and submissions.

How do I connect techniques instead of trying isolated moves?

Choose a starting position, one attack and one response to a common defense. Drill that small sequence, then let your partner choose whether to defend so you learn when to switch rather than forcing the first move. Plan a focused session.

Why does a technique work in drills but fail during rolling?

Cooperative drills make the starting grips and timing predictable. Rolling adds movement and resistance. Bridge the gap with positional rounds from the relevant setup and gradually make the partner’s reactions less predictable. Explore training methods.

What is positional sparring?

You start in a specific position with agreed goals, such as passing versus retaining guard. Reset after an escape, pass or other agreed outcome so both partners get repeated chances to solve the same problem. Use the training guide.

What is flow rolling?

Flow rolling is cooperative movement with lighter resistance and an emphasis on transitions. Agree on the pace with your partner first; it works best when both people allow movement rather than turning it into a hard round. Explore training methods.

How can I make drilling less repetitive?

Keep the same skill but vary one detail: the starting grip, the partner’s posture or a specific defensive response. Add resistance gradually and keep a clear goal so the variation still trains the technique. Build a practice plan.

How do I choose a useful goal for a round?

Pick one action you can recognize, such as recovering a knee inside, securing an underhook or maintaining control after a pass. Judge the round by your decisions and attempts as well as its final outcome. Set a training focus.

What should I write down after class?

Record the position you studied, one detail that helped, one recurring problem and a question to ask next time. A short note you revisit is more useful than a long list of moves you never practice. Visit the training page.

How can I practice BJJ when I do not have a partner?

Use the Training page for solo movement drills, review a video and rehearse the order of a technique’s steps. Solo practice can reinforce movement, but timing and resistance still need partner work. Find solo drills.

How do I use instructional videos without getting overwhelmed?

Choose one problem from your recent training, watch a lesson that addresses it and bring one or two details to practice. Revisit the lesson after trying it rather than collecting unrelated moves. Choose a video lesson.

How do I ask a coach a better technique question?

Describe the position, the grip or movement you tried and your partner’s reaction. For example, “When I recover my knee from side control, they block my hip—what should I change?” gives your coach a specific problem to inspect. Contact the creator about the site.

Competition & this site

How do I prepare for my first BJJ competition?

Check your event’s current rules and weigh-in details, pack your required gear, and plan how you will warm up and get to the venue. Use the competition checklist.

How do I find a specific technique video on this site?

Search the video library by technique name or a related term, then select Watch video. Category filters narrow the results, and Pick a lesson chooses a video from the entries currently showing. Open the video library.

What is the difference between the daily puzzle and training puzzles?

The daily game gives visitors the same day’s challenge. Training puzzles let you practice additional BJJ Connections rounds by belt level, including brown belt, without waiting for tomorrow’s puzzle. Try training puzzles.

Do the technique belt levels mean other belts cannot practice them?

The site’s belt levels organize learning difficulty; they are not a universal syllabus or a competition permission list. Use your coach’s guidance to choose suitable techniques and check your event’s rules separately. Browse belt-level techniques.

The Belt System

Belt progression in BJJ is based on time, knowledge, and competition performance. Requirements vary by association.

BeltTypical DurationFocus
White1-2 yearsFundamentals: basic positions, escapes, and submissions
Blue2-3 yearsBuilding a game: developing a personal style and refining technique
Purple2-4 yearsAdvanced techniques: guard specialization, competition strategy
Brown1-3 yearsMastery: refining details, teaching ability, competition at high level
BlackLifetimeExpertise: deep understanding of all aspects of BJJ
Note: Timeframes are general guidelines only. Each association (IBJJF, CBJJ, etc.) has its own requirements. Belt progression depends on attendance, technique, competition results, and instructor assessment.
